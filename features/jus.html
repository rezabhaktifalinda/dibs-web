YOU CAN'T CREATE TASTE

An AI art collector on his mum's filo pastry, his first wrong buy, the places he'd send a friend, and why even the strongest AI model can't create taste.

Interview and words: Dudu

Hero photo: Jus, calm daytime portrait

I've known Jus since before Dibs had a name. He's the one who invited me to Silk Road when it came to Bali.

When I was doubting whether I could start from zero again, he was one of the people who backed me to build Dibs. So, full disclosure: he's a friend. I asked him anyway, because he's spent years answering the question Dibs asks: what's actually worth it?

His name is Jusuf. "My friends call me Jus," he says. Mainly, he collects art, much of it made with AI. He used to run SILK, the art house behind Silk Road. "Recently, we were the youngest gallery to be exhibiting at Art Basel Hong Kong." [confirm with Jus: he said "back in May", but the 2026 fair ran 27–29 March] He says he's in a transition period in his career. "But I definitely want to make a name for myself."

So I didn't start with machines. I asked what was on his family's table. Within a minute we were on his mum's filo pastry. A few minutes later he was telling me no AI model can create taste.

Made with love

He was born in Macedonia and raised in London, in a family of Albanian descent. The table he describes sounds a lot like a Turkish breakfast: filo pastry [byrek — confirm], spinach, feta, lamb stew, beef stew. "Probably not the healthiest food," he says, "but very delicious." And beans. "Not English breakfast beans," he says. "Big red beans, with garlic and onions, sometimes meat."

I admit I'm still learning to love the red beans. "You need to come and try my mum's," he says. I've promised I will.

Growing up wasn't easy, and things weren't easy to get. A lot of what he wore and ate was handmade. His mum cooked everything from scratch. His grandma made his jumpers and his T-shirts. When I ask what "good" meant to his parents, he thinks about it first. "Good always meant accessible," he says, and then: "From their perspective, good is made with love."

Food taught him taste before art did. His brother is basically a chef, and Jus grew up on his cooking, surrounded in London by lots of cultures, especially African and Turkish, where seasoning is a big thing.

"Regardless of whether you have the strongest AI model or the best mathematical genius, you can't create taste," he says. "It's a makeup of all your experiences as a human being." When he sees people cooking with all their measuring stuff out, he says, that's not the authentic way. "The authentic way of cooking is how the aunties and the mums cook. They just have an idea in their head."

FROM THEIR PERSPECTIVE, GOOD IS MADE WITH LOVE

The first mistake

Ask Jus what good art is and he says it's really hard to answer. Ask him about the first piece he got wrong, though, and he can tell you exactly why.

His first piece was bought to make money. "I told myself a lie that I really liked the art. Actually, I bought it because I thought it was going to go up in price, and it didn't. It was no fault of the artist. My intentions just weren't pure." He still buys for money sometimes. But whatever it is, "buy it because you genuinely want it, not because someone else told you to."

He didn't grow up around art. As a teenager he started an Instagram page about street art and grew it from zero to about 25,000 followers, posting every single day. "It was escapism," he says. "Just to feel better about my life." He buys art the way he started buying crypto in 2016: for a vision of where the world will go, not for what other collectors like. "They have their own story. I have mine."

Most of what he buys now is by AI artists, work he thinks people will appreciate in the future. He also collects Samantha Cavet, a friend of us both, so I ask about her. She's a photographer and digital painter he met in Paris in 2023. He liked her work before they met, and more after, because they'd had a very similar childhood. "In a world where you can generate a photograph in seconds, I think she'll be seen as one of the last waves of human photographers showing what life really looks like." Lately she's been moving into video. I tell him her videos make the work feel more alive. Dreamy, like stepping through a gate into another world. He agrees. "I'm very excited to see what she does with that," he says. "But most importantly, she's just an amazing human."

Seeing works he bought go on to be featured in museums and collected by others is what gave him confidence in his own eye. But that confidence isn't fixed. "To be honest with you, I'm always battling with this every day. One day I wake up and think I know what I'm doing. The next day I think I have no idea." Everyone goes through that, he says.

Trying too hard

When I ask what makes him walk away from a place, he says: "This is actually really easy. When they try too hard."

He can see it in hotels and restaurants alike: the interior that hasn't been touched in years, the nervous staff, the menu that "looks like it's been generated on ChatGPT," with dishes "you've seen several times in several other places." "You pick up on the small things," he says, "and you know this hasn't been built with love. In food and in art, you can't build a business like that. There has to be soul behind it." In Bangkok, he says, it's the opposite: "really old tables and furniture," but "you can feel the soul."

Earlier that day, he'd found the opposite in Ubud. At [VENUE — confirm], a Mediterranean breakfast place, the fried egg came on diced lamb shoulder instead of the usual avocado. "I'd never seen that dish before, so they obviously haven't copied it from somewhere else. It came from someone's head. I'll go back just because I don't know who else is making it."

"Most Bali menus are the same," he says. "It's really not hard to stand out." I tell him I feel the same: every time I go to a restaurant in Bali, it's like they're copying another menu.

THERE HAS TO BE SOUL BEHIND IT

Jus's three rules for knowing what's worth it

  1. Get to know the person behind it.
  2. Check they look after the people they work with.
  3. Make sure you haven't seen it somewhere else.

And if you're starting with no money and no connections? His answer, he admits, sounds a bit silly. "Build a context file of yourself. Write about your life: your childhood, your favourite films, your favourite brands. Put it into AI, and you'll get a clear picture of who you are." Your taste, he says, is the sum of what you've lived. Then the good news: artists sell directly on Instagram now, no gallery, no middleman. "You can start with five or ten dollars."

Taste and money

When we get to Bali, he puts it like this.

THE PROBLEM WITH BALI, AND WITH ART IN GENERAL, IS THAT THE PEOPLE WITH TASTE DON'T HAVE MONEY, AND THE PEOPLE THAT HAVE MONEY DON'T HAVE TASTE

He means the restaurants being built by people with a lot of money and the same idea, "bringing nothing to Bali that it needs." Then he turns to the other half. Bali is rich in talent, especially Indonesians from all over the country, who may not have the money to build but have the know-how and the taste. "The coolest people in Bali are the Indonesians, rather than us bules."

In 2025, a silver jewellery maker and a carpenter came to his house. Their Instagram pages weren't huge, but the quality was "good enough to work for a huge brand." He doesn't think he'd have that kind of access in Europe.

It's one of the reasons I started Dibs: the places and people worth knowing that nobody has told you about yet. "Which," he says, "is hopefully what you can help with."

And what Bali gets right? After two years here, he's come to appreciate Balinese culture: the parades, the dancing, the ceremonies. "They're very, very proud of their culture," he says, and he thinks we should amplify that through museums, galleries and music events, "and a cultural institution can literally just mean a restaurant." Don't force culture, he says. Give it a platform. A lot of the new event spaces feel forced, not part of Bali.

His Bali

When his best friend came to Bali on honeymoon in September, Jus sent him to Begawan Biji, a farm-to-table restaurant in Payangan, near Ubud. It's run, he says, by Joshua [confirm Joshua's role and surname]. "When I talk about food made with love, that's the place." Joshua sits down and explains how it all works, the staff look after you, and the farming keeps the locals in mind. "It makes you feel as if you're tasting a bit of Bali."

When he wants to be alone, he goes to Jiwa Garden [confirm with Jus: the Canggu one?], which he describes as a shack in the middle of the forest, where they sometimes hold art exhibitions, do yoga, and keep chickens and a few other animals. "It's not super glamorous. It's just peaceful." There are lots of places like it in Bali, he says. Not many people know about them.

And overrated? The most expensive places, and a couple that have opened in the last year in the style of Instagram influencers: big mirrors, neon lights, dancing on tables. "That's the antithesis of what Bali should become."
